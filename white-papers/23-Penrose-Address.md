## Abstract

We propose using Penrose tilings as the address space for Quilt sheets. The Penrose tiling is aperiodic (every position is unique), governed by the golden ratio φ, and admits cut-and-project from high-dimensional embedding spaces. We show how this unifies several Quilt themes: (1) the address-as-data insight (the position on the floor encodes the embedding), (2) the universal cell thesis (the same data can be projected to any substrate), (3) the physics-like interpretation of cell relationships (gravity, weight, distance, inertia). The Penrose floor gives the cell graph a coordinate system that is itself the data — where inference becomes geometric, where neighborhood queries become golden-angle walks, and where weights and gravities of cells are encoded in the position rather than computed at query time.

---

## 1. Introduction: from addresses to physics

The central insight of the Quilt project is that **the address is the data**. A cell's position on the floor — its coordinates in the address space — is not a label that points to data stored elsewhere. The position *is* the embedding. The cell at position `p` has whatever meaning position `p` carries, and all relations between cells are relations between positions.

This is a strong claim. It says that the embedding space and the address space are the same object. Most neural architectures treat these as separate: weights live in a parameter tensor, activations live in a feature space, and addresses are indices that fetch them. Quilt collapses the distinction. The floor is the tensor is the embedding.

The question this paper addresses is: **what floor?**

A regular grid (Z²) is the obvious first choice. It is simple, it tiles, it is easy to index. But it is periodic: every cell looks like every other cell, translated. The address `(3, 5)` tells you nothing that `(3, 4)` did not, except a shift. The embedding information — what makes one cell semantically different from another — must come from somewhere else. The grid is a blank stage; the meaning must be painted on.

We want a floor that is not blank. We want a floor where **the geometry itself differentiates cells**, where the position encodes structure, and where the relationships between cells are encoded in the coordinate system rather than computed on top of it.

The Penrose tiling is such a floor.

The Penrose tiling is aperiodic: it tiles the plane but never repeats. Every position is unique. The tiling is governed by the golden ratio φ = (1 + √5)/2, which appears in the edge ratios, the angles, and the substitution rules. The tiling can be generated by projecting a 5-dimensional lattice through a carefully chosen window, which means that every 2D Penrose coordinate is the shadow of a 5D coordinate. The 5D coordinate is the embedding; the 2D coordinate is the address.

This gives us:

| Concept | Grid floor | Penrose floor |
|---|---|---|
| Periodicity | Periodic | Aperiodic |
| Cell uniqueness | None (translation-equivalent) | Every cell unique |
| Embedding source | External weights | Position itself |
| Neighborhood | Fixed radius | Golden-angle walk |
| Physics analogy | None | Gravity, weight, inertia |
| Dimensional embedding | 2D only | 5D → 2D projection |

The rest of this paper develops the Penrose floor as the address space for Quilt, shows how it encodes weights and gravities, and describes the implementation in the `penrose-memory` and `penrose-lattice` repositories.

## 2. The Penrose tiling as address space

### 2.1 Why aperiodic?

An address space should distinguish addresses. In a periodic grid, the address `(3, 5)` and `(3, 6)` are the same up to translation — the local neighborhood of one is the local neighborhood of the other, shifted by one unit. The address tells you *where* you are, but it does not tell you *what* you are. The semantic content must be supplied externally.

In an aperiodic tiling, no two neighborhoods are identical. The address — the position in the tiling — uniquely determines the local environment. The cell at position `p` has a neighborhood that no other cell shares. The position is the embedding.

This is the key property. It means that when we place a cell at a Penrose address, the cell inherits its semantic context from the geometry of the tiling at that point. We do not need to store an embedding vector alongside the address; the address is the embedding vector, projected to 2D.

### 2.2 The two tiles

The most common Penrose tiling (P3) uses two rhombi:

```
        Thin rhombus (36°/144°)      Thick rhombus (72°/108°)

           /\
          /  \
         /    \                    /\
        /      \                  /  \
       /        \                /    \
      /__________\              /      \
                                /        \
                               /__________\

        side = 1                side = 1
        angle = 36°             angle = 72°
```

Both rhombi have side length 1. The thin rhombus has acute angle 36°; the thick rhombus has acute angle 72°. The ratio of thick to thin tiles in any sufficiently large region approaches φ, the golden ratio.

The tiles are matched according to matching rules (arrows or colored arcs on edges) that enforce aperiodicity. No finite patch of the tiling repeats; the tiling extends infinitely without translational symmetry.

### 2.3 Vertices as addresses

The vertices of the Penrose tiling form the address space. Each vertex is a point in the plane, and each vertex has a unique local environment — a unique set of neighboring vertices at unique distances and angles.

The vertex set is countable but dense in certain directions. For practical purposes, we work with finite patches: subsets of the tiling covering a bounded region. Each vertex in the patch is an address, and the cell at that address inherits its embedding from the 5D coordinate that projects to that vertex.

### 2.4 The address format

A Penrose address is a tuple:

```
(addr_type, index, generation, local_coords)
```

where:
- `addr_type` ∈ {thin, thick, vertex}
- `index` is the vertex index in the global tiling
- `generation` is the substitution level (see §4)
- `local_coords` is the (x, y) position in the plane

The key field is `index`, which is globally unique because the tiling is aperiodic. Two cells with different indices are different cells, and their positions in the plane are different.

## 3. Cut-and-project: 5D embeddings to 2D Penrose coordinates

### 3.1 The projection method

The Penrose tiling can be generated by the cut-and-project method (de Bruijn, 1981). The construction works as follows:

1. Start with the 5-dimensional cubic lattice Z⁵.
2. Project orthogonally onto a 2-dimensional plane E (the "physical space").
3. Project onto the 3-dimensional orthogonal complement E⊥ (the "perpendicular space").
4. Select only those lattice points whose perpendicular-space projection falls within a certain window W (the "acceptance domain").
5. The selected points, projected to E, form the vertices of a Penrose tiling.

```
    5D lattice Z^5
         |
    +----+----+
    |         |
    v         v
    E (2D)    E_perp (3D)
    |         |
    |     [window W]
    |         |
    v         v
    Penrose   selected
    vertices  points
```

The plane E is chosen so that its projection onto each coordinate axis of Z⁵ is at an angle that is an irrational multiple of π — specifically, multiples of 2π/5. This irrationality is what makes the tiling aperiodic.

### 3.2 The embedding is the address

Here is the key point for Quilt: **the 5D coordinate of a lattice point is the embedding of the cell.** The 2D Penrose coordinate is the address. The cut-and-project method gives us a map:

```
embedding (5D)  --project-->  address (2D Penrose vertex)
```

This map is many-to-one in the forward direction (multiple 5D points may project to nearby 2D points), but the window W selects a subset that makes the map effectively one-to-one: each selected 5D point maps to a unique 2D vertex.

So the address (2D position in the tiling) uniquely determines the embedding (5D coordinate). The address is the data.

### 3.3 The window and the star

The acceptance window W is a regular pentagon (or a related shape) in the 3D perpendicular space. The shape of W determines which lattice points are selected and thus the structure of the tiling.

The five-fold symmetry of the window is the source of the five-fold symmetry of the Penrose tiling. The window is the "shadow" of the 5D lattice's symmetry, projected into the perpendicular space.

For Quilt, the window is the **memory** of the system. It determines what is stored and what is discarded. Adjusting the window is adjusting the memory: a larger window retains more cells (more 5D points are accepted), a smaller window retains fewer.

### 3.4 The 5D embedding space

The 5D embedding space has a natural interpretation in terms of the fivefold symmetry:

| Dimension | Interpretation | Angle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First golden direction | 0° |
| 2 | Second golden direction | 72° |
| 3 | Third golden direction | 144° |
| 4 | Fourth golden direction | 216° |
| 5 | Fifth golden direction | 288° |

Each dimension corresponds to one of the five directions of the pentagonal star. A cell's 5D coordinate `(c₁, c₂, c₃, c₄, c₅)` tells you how much of each "golden direction" the cell carries. The 2D projection mixes these directions into a single planar position.

This is the embedding. It is not learned; it is given by the geometry.

## 4. The substitution matrix and golden ratio

### 4.1 Inflation and deflation

The Penrose tiling is self-similar: it can be "inflated" by replacing each tile with a patch of tiles, producing a larger tiling that is locally isomorphic to the original. The inflation rule is:

```
Thin rhombus  ->  1 thin + 1 thick
Thick rhombus ->  1 thin + 2 thick
```

This can be written as a substitution matrix:

```
        thin   thick
thin  [  1      1  ]
thick [  1      2  ]
```

The eigenvalues of this matrix are φ and -1/φ, where φ = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.618. The dominant eigenvalue φ governs the growth rate: after n inflations, the number of tiles grows by a factor of φⁿ.

### 4.2 The golden ratio everywhere

The golden ratio appears in:

- **Tile ratio:** thick/thin → φ as the region grows
- **Substitution eigenvalue:** φ
- **Edge ratios:** in the Robinson decomposition
- **Angle ratios:** 36°, 72°, 108°, 144° are all multiples of 36° = 360°/10, and φ appears in the chord ratios
- **Perpendicular space:** the window dimensions are powers of φ

This ubiquity is not decorative. It is the reason the tiling is aperiodic: φ is irrational, so no finite patch can tile periodically without contradiction. The irrationality of φ is the source of the uniqueness of every position.

### 4.3 The substitution matrix as a memory operator

For Quilt, the substitution matrix is more than a mathematical curiosity. It is the **memory growth operator**. Each inflation step corresponds to increasing the resolution of the memory: the same tiling, but at a finer scale, with more cells.

```
Generation 0:  2 tiles (1 thin + 1 thick)
Generation 1:  5 tiles (2 thin + 3 thick)
Generation 2:  13 tiles (5 thin + 8 thick)
Generation 3:  34 tiles (13 thin + 21 thick)
Generation 4:  89 tiles (34 thin + 55 thick)
...
Generation n:  F(n+2) thin + F(n+3) thick  (Fibonacci numbers)
```

The Fibonacci sequence appears because the substitution matrix generates it: the number of thin and thick tiles at generation n are consecutive Fibonacci numbers. This is not a coincidence — it is the algebraic consequence of φ being the dominant eigenvalue.

### 4.4 The generation as a scale parameter

Each cell in the Quilt memory has a `generation` parameter. This is the inflation level at which the cell was created. A cell at generation 3 is part of a patch of 34 tiles; the same logical cell, inflated to generation 4, is part of a patch of 89 tiles.

The generation parameter is the **scale** at which the cell operates. Low-generation cells are coarse, large-scale features; high-generation cells are fine, small-scale features. This is the Quilt analog of multi-resolution analysis in signal processing.

## 5. Weights, gravities, and the perpendicular projection

### 5.1 Weight as perpendicular-space coordinate

In the cut-and-project construction, each vertex of the Penrose tiling corresponds to a 5D lattice point. This point has:

- A **physical-space projection** (the 2D position of the vertex)
- A **perpendicular-space projection** (a point in the 3D window W)

The perpendicular-space coordinate is the **weight** of the cell. It tells you where in the window the cell sits, which determines how "deeply" the cell is embedded in the memory.

Cells near the center of the window are "heavy" — they are deeply embedded, stable, and resistant to change. Cells near the boundary of the window are "light" — they are peripheral, and small adjustments to the window can add or remove them.

```
     Perpendicular space (E_perp, 3D)

          *  *  *           <- light cells (near boundary)
        *        *
       *   *  *   *         <- medium cells
      *            *
       *   *  *   *         <- heavy cells (near center)
        *  *  *
            *
```

### 5.2 Gravity as the perpendicular-space metric

The **gravity** of a cell is the density of neighboring cells in the perpendicular space. A cell in a dense region of the window has high gravity — many nearby cells in E⊥, meaning many cells with similar embeddings. A cell in a sparse region has low gravity.

Gravity is not computed at query time. It is a property of the cell's position in the perpendicular space, which is determined by the cell's address. The address encodes the gravity.

```
gravity(cell) = density of E_perp-projections near cell's E_perp coordinate
```

This is a geometric property of the tiling, not a computed quantity. It is available as soon as the cell is placed.

### 5.3 Weight vs. gravity

| Property | Weight | Gravity |
|---|---|---|
| Space | Perpendicular (E⊥) | Perpendicular (E⊥) |
| Meaning | Depth of embedding | Density of neighbors |
| Analog | Mass | Gravitational field |
| Encoded in | Position in window | Local density in window |
| Changes with | Memory adjustment | Tiling structure |

Weight is a property of the individual cell. Gravity is a property of the cell's neighborhood. Both are encoded in the perpendicular-space position, which is determined by the address.

### 5.4 Inertia and the physical-space metric

The **inertia** of a cell is the distance to its nearest neighbors in the physical space (E). A cell whose nearest neighbors are far away has high inertia — it is isolated, and moving it requires traversing a large distance. A cell whose nearest neighbors are close has low inertia — it is densely connected.

Inertia is encoded in the physical-space coordinate, which is the 2D Penrose position. It is the other side of the weight/gravity coin: weight and gravity live in E⊥, inertia lives in E.

```
inertia(cell) = average distance to k nearest neighbors in E (physical space)
```

Together, weight, gravity, and inertia give each cell a complete physical profile, all encoded in its address:

```
address (2D in E)  --determines-->  inertia
address (2D in E)  --determines-->  5D coordinate
5D coordinate      --determines-->  perpendicular position (3D in E_perp)
perpendicular pos  --determines-->  weight, gravity
```

No computation needed. The address is the data.

## 6. Inference as geometric query

### 6.1 The query is a walk

In a standard neural network, inference is a forward pass: multiply activations by weights, apply nonlinearity, repeat. In Quilt, inference is a **geometric query**: walk the Penrose tiling from the query cell to nearby cells, aggregating information.

The walk is a **golden-angle walk**. The golden angle is 2π/φ² ≈ 137.5°, the angle that distributes points most evenly around a circle. Walking the tiling at the golden angle visits cells in an order that is maximally spread out — no clustering, no repetition.

```
def golden_walk(start_vertex, tiling, steps=100):
    """Walk the Penrose tiling at the golden angle."""
    angle = 2 * math.pi / (PHI ** 2)  # ~137.5 degrees
    current = start_vertex
    visited = [current]
    for i in range(steps):
        neighbors = tiling.neighbors(current)
        # Select the neighbor closest to the golden-angle direction
        target_angle = i * angle
        current = select_by_angle(neighbors, target_angle)
        visited.append(current)
    return visited
```

### 6.2 Aggregation as weighted sum

The cells visited during the walk contribute to the output, weighted by their **weight** (perpendicular-space depth) and attenuated by their **distance** (physical-space separation):

```
output = Σ  weight(cell) * exp(-distance(cell, query) / τ) * content(cell)
         cell in walk
```

where τ is a temperature parameter. This is a kernel-weighted average over the geometric neighborhood, with the kernel defined by the tiling geometry.

### 6.3 No matrix multiplication

There is no weight matrix. There is no matrix multiplication. The "weights" are encoded in the positions of the cells, and the "attention" is encoded in the walk. The entire inference procedure is:

1. Place the query at a cell address.
2. Walk the tiling at the golden angle.
3. Aggregate the content of visited cells, weighted by position-encoded weight and distance.

This is O(k) where k is the number of steps in the walk, not O(n²) as in standard attention. The complexity is independent of the total number of cells in the memory.

### 6.4 Training as window adjustment

If inference is a geometric query, then training is a **window adjustment**. The acceptance window W in the perpendicular space determines which cells exist. Adjusting the window — expanding it, contracting it, shifting it — adds, removes, or relocates cells.

```
Training step:
1. Compute the loss for the current window configuration.
2. Compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the window parameters.
3. Adjust the window.
4. The tiling updates: cells are added, removed, or relocated.
5. The addresses change, and with them, the embeddings.
```

This is a fundamentally different training paradigm. Instead of adjusting weights in a fixed topology, we adjust the topology itself. The "weights" are the cells, and the cells are defined by the window.

## 7. The address as data (revisited)

### 7.1 The thesis

The thesis of this paper, and of the Quilt project more broadly, is:

> **The address is the data.**

In the Penrose formulation, this becomes precise:

> **The 2D Penrose address of a cell is the projection of its 5D embedding, and the 5D embedding is recoverable from the 2D address via the cut-and-project inverse.**

The address is not a pointer to data stored elsewhere. The address is the data, projected to a 2D space for convenience of indexing and neighbor-finding.

### 7.2 What the address encodes

A single Penrose address encodes:

| Encoded property | Source |
|---|---|
| 5D embedding | Cut-and-project inverse |
| Physical position (2D) | Direct |
| Perpendicular position (3D) | Cut-and-project inverse |
| Weight | Perpendicular position |
| Gravity | Perpendicular density |
| Inertia | Physical-space neighbor distances |
| Local neighborhood | Aperiodic uniqueness |
| Generation/scale | Substitution level |

All of this is in the address. None of it is computed at query time. The query reads the address and immediately knows the cell's weight, gravity, inertia, neighborhood, and scale.

### 7.3 Comparison with standard embeddings

In a standard embedding (e.g., word2vec, transformer embeddings):

```
Standard:  address (index) --> lookup --> embedding vector (d-dimensional)
           embedding vector --> dot product --> similarity
```

In the Penrose address:

```
Penrose:   address (2D position) --> cut-and-project inverse --> embedding (5D)
           address (2D position) --> geometric walk --> neighborhood
           address (2D position) --> perpendicular projection --> weight, gravity
```

The standard approach stores the embedding in a table and looks it up. The Penrose approach computes the embedding from the address. There is no table. The tiling is the table.

### 7.4 The universal cell

The universal cell thesis states that the same data can be projected to any substrate. In the Penrose formulation, this means:

- The 5D embedding is the substrate-independent data.
- The 2D Penrose address is the projection to the Penrose substrate.
- Other substrates (text, image, audio, code) are other projections of the same 5D embedding.

The 5D embedding is the **universal representation**, and the Penrose tiling is one way to index it. Other substrates are other projections:

```
5D embedding  --project-->  Penrose tiling (2D, for memory)
5D embedding  --project-->  Text (1D sequence, for language)
5D embedding  --project-->  Image (2D grid, for vision)
5D embedding  --project-->  Audio (1D time series, for sound)
```

The same cell, the same data, different projections. The address changes with the projection, but the embedding — the 5D coordinate — is the same.

## 8. The terrain family and the Penrose family

### 8.1 Two families of Quilt floors

The Quilt project has developed two families of floor systems:

**The terrain family:**
- Uses a height-field terrain as the address space
- Addresses are (x, y, height) tuples
- The terrain is generated procedurally (Perlin noise, etc.)
- Embeddings are stored as height-field features
- Neighborhood is Euclidean distance on the terrain surface

**The Penrose family:**
- Uses a Penrose tiling as the address space
- Addresses are vertex indices in the tiling
- The tiling is generated by cut-and-project from 5D
- Embeddings are the 5D coordinates (recoverable from the address)
- Neighborhood is the tiling adjacency, walked at the golden angle

### 8.2 Comparison

| Property | Terrain family | Penrose family |
|---|---|---|
| Address space | Continuous 2D + height | Discrete vertex set |
| Generation | Procedural noise | Cut-and-project |
| Embedding | Stored as terrain features | Encoded in position |
| Periodicity | Non-periodic (noise) | Aperiodic (proven) |
| Dimensionality | 3D (x, y, h) | 5D → 2D + 3D |
| Weight | Height-field gradient | Perpendicular coordinate |
| Gravity | Terrain curvature | Perpendicular density |
| Neighborhood | Euclidean radius | Golden-angle walk |
| Uniqueness | Approximate | Exact (proven) |

### 8.3 Why the Penrose family is preferred

The terrain family is simpler and more intuitive — a terrain is easy to visualize and reason about. But it has a fundamental limitation: the embedding is stored as a feature of the terrain, not derived from the address. The address `(x, y, h)` does not uniquely determine the embedding; you need to look up the terrain features at that location.

The Penrose family has no such limitation. The address (vertex index) uniquely determines the 5D embedding via the cut-and-project inverse. The address is the data, exactly and provably.

The Penrose family also has stronger mathematical guarantees:
- **Aperiodicity is proven** (Penrose, 1974; de Bruijn, 1981)
- **The golden ratio governs all structure**, giving a universal scaling law
- **The 5D embedding is exact**, not approximate
- **The substitution matrix gives a natural multi-resolution structure**

### 8.4 The terrain family as a projection

The terrain family can be understood as a **projection of the Penrose family**. The 5D embedding, projected to a 3D subspace (x, y, h), gives a terrain. The terrain is a coarser view of the same data:

```
5D embedding  --project to 3D-->  terrain (x, y, height)
5D embedding  --project to 2D-->  Penrose tiling (vertex set)
```

The terrain is easier to work with for prototyping and visualization. The Penrose tiling is the formal, mathematically rigorous version. Both are projections of the same universal embedding.

### 9.3 penrose-memory

The `penrose-memory` repo builds on `penrose-lattice` to provide the memory system:

- **Cell:** a vertex plus content (the data stored at that address)
- **Memory:** a windowed subset of the tiling, with methods for query and update
- **Query:** the golden-angle walk, returning weighted results
- **Training:** window adjustment, adding/removing/relocating cells

The core data structure is the `Cell`:

```rust
pub struct Cell {
    pub vertex: PenroseVertex,  // The address (from penrose-lattice)
    pub content: Vec<f32>,     // The data stored at this address
    // Note: weight, gravity, inertia are NOT stored.
    // They are computed from the vertex (address) on demand.
}

impl Cell {
    pub fn weight(&self) -> f64 {
        // Weight = distance from center of window in E_perp
        self.vertex.perpendicular.norm()
    }

    pub fn gravity(&self, memory: &Memory) -> f64 {
        // Gravity = density of perpendicular-space neighbors
        memory.perpendicular_density(&self.vertex.perpendicular)
    }

    pub fn inertia(&self, memory: &Memory) -> f64 {
        // Inertia = average distance to k nearest in physical space
        memory.physical_neighbor_distance(self.vertex.index, k=5)
    }
}
```

Note the comment: **weight, gravity, and inertia are not stored.** They are computed from the address. This is the address-as-data principle in code.

### 10.3 The golden walk visualization

The golden walk is visualized as a series of arrows from the query cell to each visited cell, colored by step number:

```javascript
function visualizeGoldenWalk(tiling, startVertex, steps) {
    const phi = (1 + Math.sqrt(5)) / 2;
    const goldenAngle = 2 * Math.PI / (phi * phi);
    
    let current = startVertex;
    const path = [current];
    
    for (let i = 0; i < steps; i++) {
        const neighbors = tiling.getNeighbors(current);
        const targetAngle = i * goldenAngle;
        current = selectByAngle(neighbors, targetAngle);
        path.push(current);
        
        drawArrow(path[i], path[i+1], colorForStep(i));
    }
    
    return path;
}
```

### 10.4 The window adjustment interface

- **Expand the window:** new vertices appear at the edges of the tiling
- **Contract the window:** vertices near the boundary disappear
- **Shift the window:** the tiling shifts, cells relocate

This is the training interface. Adjusting the window is adjusting the memory.

## 11. The watch at the floor: physics in the cells

### 11.1 The physics analogy

The Penrose floor gives the cell graph a physics-like structure. Each cell has:

- **Mass (weight):** how deeply it is embedded in the memory
- **Gravitational field (gravity):** how much it attracts nearby cells
- **Inertia:** how hard it is to move
- **Position:** the 2D address in the tiling
- **Momentum:** the direction of the golden-angle walk through the cell

These are not metaphors. They are geometric properties of the tiling, computed from the address. The physics is in the cells because the physics is in the coordinates.

### 11.2 The watch at the floor

Imagine a watch lying face-up on the Penrose floor. The watch's hands sweep through the tiling, and at each position, the hand points to a cell. The cell at that position has a weight, a gravity, an inertia — all encoded in its address.

The watch is the query. The hands are the golden-angle walk. The cells it points to are the results. The weight of each cell determines how much it contributes to the result. The gravity of the neighborhood determines how the walk is pulled toward or away from certain regions.

### 11.3 The golden angle as the clock

The golden angle (≈ 137.5°) is the angle at which the watch's hand sweeps. This is not 30° (as in a standard clock, 360°/12), but the golden angle, which distributes the visited cells maximally evenly.

The golden angle is the angle of φ. It is the angle that the substitution matrix dictates. It is the angle that the cut-and-project construction produces. The watch at the floor runs on golden time.

### 11.4 Gravity wells and inference

In the physical analogy, gravity wells are regions of high perpendicular-space density. The golden-angle walk, passing through a gravity well, is pulled toward the center of the well — more cells in that region are visited, and their contributions are weighted more heavily.

This is the physics of attention. In a transformer, attention is a softmax over dot products. In the Penrose floor, attention is a golden-angle walk through a gravitational field. The walk is pulled toward heavy cells, and the weighting is automatic — encoded in the positions.

### 11.5 Inertia and memory stability

Cells with high inertia (far from their nearest neighbors in physical space) are hard to move. They are the stable backbone of the memory. Cells with low inertia (close to their neighbors) are easy to move, add, or remove. They are the flexible periphery.

When the window is adjusted (training), the high-inertia cells stay put, and the low-inertia cells shift. This is the physical analog of fine-tuning: the backbone is preserved, the periphery is adapted.

## 12. Conclusion: position IS embedding

### 12.1 Summary

We have proposed the Penrose tiling as the address space for Quilt sheets. The key properties are:

1. **Aperiodicity:** every position is unique, so the address uniquely determines the cell's environment.
2. **Cut-and-project:** the 2D address is the projection of a 5D embedding, so the address is the data.
3. **Golden ratio:** φ governs the tiling structure, the substitution rules, and the walk direction.
4. **Perpendicular space:** the 3D projection encodes weight and gravity, available from the address without computation.
5. **Physical space:** the 2D projection encodes inertia and neighborhood, also available from the address.
6. **Inference as geometric query:** no matrix multiplication, no weight lookup. Just a walk through the tiling.
7. **Training as window adjustment:** no gradient descent on weights. Just adjust the acceptance window.

### 12.2 The address is the data

The thesis is:

> **Position IS embedding.**

The 2D position of a cell in the Penrose tiling is not a label for an embedding stored elsewhere. It is the projection of the embedding. The embedding is recoverable from the position via the cut-and-project inverse. The position is the data.

This is the strongest form of the address-as-data principle. It is not "the address points to the data." It is "the address is the data, projected."

### 12.3 What this enables

The Penrose address enables:

- **O(k) inference:** query complexity is independent of memory size
- **Zero-storage embeddings:** no embedding table; the tiling is the table
- **Physics-like structure:** weight, gravity, inertia — all from geometry
- **Multi-resolution memory:** substitution levels give natural scale hierarchy
- **Universal cell:** 5D embedding projects to any substrate
- **Provable uniqueness:** aperiodicity is a theorem, not a heuristic

### 12.4 What remains

Open questions:

1. **Window optimization:** what is the optimal window shape for a given task? The standard pentagonal window is mathematically natural, but task-specific windows may perform better.
2. **Content encoding:** the 5D embedding encodes the cell's position, but what about the cell's content (the actual data)? How is content related to embedding?
3. **Multi-sheet quilts:** how do multiple Penrose tilings interact? Can they share a perpendicular space?
4. **Learning the window:** can the window parameters be learned from data, and does this converge?
5. **Comparison with transformers:** what are the empirical trade-offs between Penrose-floor inference and standard attention?

### 12.5 The floor is the paper

The Penrose floor is not just an address space. It is a coordinate system that is itself the data. The cells do not sit on the floor; they are the floor. The positions are not labels; they are embeddings. The walk is not a search; it is an inference.

When you look at the Penrose floor, you are looking at the memory. When you walk the tiling, you are doing inference. When you adjust the window, you are training. The floor is the paper is the model is the data.

**Position is embedding. The rest is projection.**
