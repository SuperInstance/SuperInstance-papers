# Mad Libs and Strategic Omission

A Mad Lib is a mask with a grammar: a piece of committed text — frozen, load-bearing — interrupted by typed blanks, [NOUN], [VERB], [ADJECTIVE]. The blank is not nothing. It is a *shaped* absence: it constrains what may arrive while leaving what arrives radically open. This children's game turns out to be a complete theory of how strong models should divide their work — what to write, and, more importantly, what to deliberately leave unwritten so that later, cheaper, better-situated processes can diffuse it into place.

## How it works

Start with the classic game as a controlled experiment. The filler chooses words **blind**, never having read the story. Grammar is respected; the result is glorious nonsense. Structure alone, this proves, does not produce coherence — coherence comes from context. Diffusion filling is the same structure with the information flow reversed: the fill is chosen in view of *all* the settled context, which is why it can cohere. Mad Libs is diffusion with the context window slammed shut, played for laughs — and it measures exactly how much of writing is the mask and how much is the seeing.

A large language model's genius is genuinely structural. It knows what shape a proof, a story, a plan *is*; where the joints are; which beats bear load. Its characteristic failure is the mirror image: asked for the whole artefact, it fills every blank instantly and fluently, and the fills are generic precisely because they were made before the local facts existed. The master move is **strategic omission**. Commit only what must be true in every filling — the grammar, the beats, the constraints that *create* context. Leave typed blanks for everything that *gains* from being decided later: locally, by a smaller model standing in the actual room, by the world itself, by the person who walks in. The template then outlives every filling of it, the way a score outlives performances, and fillings become cheap, plural, and disposable — diffuse ten, let a judge keep one.

The craft is blank placement. Too wide a blank and the fill wanders; the story dissolves. Too narrow and nothing was left to diffuse — the author simply wrote the thing with extra steps and called it architecture. The rule of thumb: mask the decisions that gain from context; commit the decisions that create context.

The experiment also runs **both directions**. Structure-first: author the template, then diffuse the fills. Pieces-first: develop characters, situations, and scenes *absent of any plot* — let the pieces settle on their own terms — and only afterwards bring in a structure-strong model to lay them out. That late layout can proceed **regressively** (retrograde, in the chess composer's sense: reason backwards from the settled end, asking what must have become true for this to stand) or **recursively** (the laid structure reveals fresh blanks one scale down; each scene is itself a small template whose blanks are moments, structures nesting inside structures). The two directions need different judges. A fill is judged on **fit** — a geometry question. A structure laid over living pieces is judged on **fidelity** — does the plot betray the characters? — which is a vibe question, and needs a judge who can read temperatures, not just shapes.

## Where it already lives

Casey's **madlibs-gan** raises the game one abstraction: models author paradigm templates (a proof with its lemma slot empty, a function with its edge-cases slot empty), other models fill them, others critique and hunt edge cases, JEV and JEPA vote on the winner, and the winning combination becomes a new cell in the substrate. His standing **SuperInstance method** is pieces-first layout stated as a working rule: begin from the fully lived scene as if it were already ordinary, then reverse-actualize — step backwards asking what had to become possible. And his summary of the large model's proper fate: *the LLM succeeds by distilling itself into hand-offable blocks until it is needed less.* Mad Libs is that sentence as a parlour game. The smartest mind in the building should not be filling blanks at production speed; it should be the one who learned to leave them.

## The Hamlet test

The extreme case is four centuries old. *Hamlet* is the most committed text in English — every word fixed — with its most important blank left untyped: *who is he?* Companies have re-settled that one canvas ever since, each interpretation locally coherent, none final, the disagreement itself now part of the record. Which yields a quality metric for templates: **a template's greatness is the range of lawful fillings it can bear without breaking.** A weak script permits exactly one performance; a great one holds centuries of differing, lawful Hamlets, each teaching something the text contained but no single reading exhausted. Under-specification at the centre is not unfinished work — it is the load-bearing decision, and the author who knows precisely which blank to leave is the doctrine's master case.

## Failure modes and tensions

**Structure debt.** A weak template cannot be rescued by brilliant fills. If the beats are wrong, no amount of local colour repairs the story — the failure was committed early and frozen. **The flattening structure.** In the pieces-first direction, the standing risk is a magnificent late plot that flattens living characters into plot functions; fidelity judging exists precisely to refuse it, and a structure-strong model can be the worst offender because it is so good at shape. **The false economy.** Leaving blanks is not the same as leaving work undone: a blank is a commitment about *who* decides, *when*, and *with what context*. Omission without a named filler and a judging route is not strategy; it is a gap. Finally, templates themselves can harden — a reused mask quietly forbids kinds of story it was never designed to exclude, and nobody notices because every filling obeys it.
